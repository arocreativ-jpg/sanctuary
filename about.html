[ Maker's Mark ]

Sub Sanctuary isn't just a brand and Shane Martin isn't just the artist behind it. He's a designer, fabricator, student and curator; all interwoven and intrinsically connected. He denotes the landmarks of his journey through this world with a diverse and kinetic array of inspired artifacts — melding hand, heart and machine to create items that speak their meaning through medium, movement and utility.

Driven by the unseen forces that decide wood grain can appear as rock and water can sculpt either in near perfect symmetry — met with a daily devotion to the act of creating itself — his motives and inspiration change day by day and minute by minute. Yet one thing remains: an unwavering desire to bring himself and his fellow man all that much closer to the land and its textures that hold our sincerest meaning and memories in this living world.

Process

01 DESIGN

Every piece starts as a vector. Around a hundred designs in the library, drawn over a decade.

02 LASER

Cut and engraved. The depth and the detail happen here.

03 INLAY

Pieces set into the surface, glued up, locked together — parquetry.

04 FINISH

Sanded smooth, then coated. Soft, smooth and long-lasting — a microscopic layer that fills the grain and holds up for years.

05 SIGN

Hand signed. Numbered if it's a limited edition. Maker’s mark engraved on the back.

Materials + Sourcing

Three woods, picked for a reason.

Maple, cherry and walnut. All three are always in stock, always abundant, and the most sustainable options available to us. We don't work in oak — it isn't a good engraving surface. Mahogany is beautiful, but once you put oil on it, it goes extremely dark and the contrast is gone. Exotic woods on request.

[ A little loop of reuse ]

Everything is sourced locally — regionally, within a two-and-a-half-hour radius. Whether it's a restaurant table or a piece of hardwood for coasters.

The wood for the coasters and all the smaller products is reclaimed from the bigger ones. We don't go buy a piece of maple to turn into coasters — it's always offcuts or scrap from a shelf job or a table job. It's part of a little loop of reuse. Even the curved triangle cut out of a round coaster becomes a pendant inlay, or an earring. There's a little fractal of reuses.

Crystals are sourced locally as well — cut and cabochoned by local people.

"That word has lost a lot of meaning over the years, but there's no reason not to use it. Now is the time more than ever to redefine it. Sustainable here means it's regionally purchased, and it always has a second use."
